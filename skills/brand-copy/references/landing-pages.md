# Landing and sales copy

Use this reference for homepages, landing pages, sales pages, heroes,
headlines, benefit sections, proof, objections, testimonials, and calls to
action.

The frameworks below are grading tools. They sharpen a message; they do not
supply the message. Start with customer evidence and product truth.

## Mine the voice of the customer

Before drafting high-leverage copy, collect the language customers already use:

- testimonials, reviews, survey answers, support conversations, and churn reasons;
- sales-call notes and onboarding interviews;
- public discussions where the intended audience describes the problem;
- messages that explain what changed after the product worked.

Build a small swipe file around:

1. the words customers use for the problem before;
2. the moment or mechanism that made the product useful;
3. the task, worry, or workaround they stopped doing;
4. the outcome they value enough to describe unprompted; and
5. objections, failed alternatives, and constraints.

Keep source language verbatim in research notes. Preserve attribution and
permission boundaries. A quote may be lightly tightened only when the edit does
not change its meaning; never manufacture a testimonial from several sources.

If customer evidence is unavailable, say so. Draft from verified product facts
and mark claims that still need proof.

## Assemble distinct directions

Create alternatives from different source insights, not one sentence rewritten
several times. Useful angles include:

- the pain or workaround;
- the desired after-state;
- the mechanism that makes the outcome possible;
- the reader's identity or job;
- the cost of the status quo; and
- a credible contrast with what they already tried.

The strongest headline is often a customer phrase lightly tightened around the
product's actual outcome. Prefer language the intended reader already uses over
internal jargon.

## Write from the reader's day

Copy that people remember usually sounds like it was written by someone who
has stood where the reader stands. Before grading, run these passes:

1. **Find the scene.** Name the physical place and moment where the product
   earns its keep: the kitchen where the quote is priced, the driveway the
   reader is leaving, the van they get back into. Put the outcome in that
   place and time ("Get paid before you leave the driveway") instead of
   describing the category ("Fast invoicing for tradespeople"). Use the
   reader's everyday nouns, not slang put on for effect.
2. **Turn the product into a loop of moments.** List the few moments the
   product actually covers, in the order they happen, and give each one a
   short headline in the reader's verbs ("Price it in the kitchen.", "They
   say yes on their phone.", "Tap Job done. Get paid."). Each body line then
   says what happens and what the reader no longer has to do.
3. **Thread one worked example.** Pick one realistic customer, job and set of
   numbers and reuse them everywhere: hero visuals, demo, notifications, FAQ.
   The figures must add up (quote, deposit, balance). A consistent example
   reads as a real product; scattered round numbers read as a mockup.
4. **Name the contrast in two beats.** Section headlines can pair what the
   product is with what the reader is tired of, taken from reviews or
   research: "Four moments. No admin at night." "Built for one person, not an
   office." Back the contrast with concrete absences ("No dispatch board, no
   timesheets, no desktop setup").
5. **Make honesty part of the voice.** Label anything not shipped as coming
   soon, say so again in plain words near the claim and in the FAQ ("Not
   yet."), and answer unknowns directly ("Pricing isn't set yet."). Keep every
   promise small enough to keep, especially next to a form ("One email when
   it's your turn. Nothing else.").
6. **Give the core action a moment of play.** When the product has one action
   the reader will repeat daily, name it warmly and, where the page allows,
   let the visitor try it ("The best button you'll press all day."). The joke
   has to point at the real action, not decorate the page.

## Grade the draft

### Headline

Make the outcome or tension concrete. An action, specific outcome, and useful
context often work well, but do not force every headline into one formula.
Avoid unsupported timeframes and performance claims.

### Opening

Give the reader a reason to continue: a recognizable problem, a precise result,
a scene, a useful contrast, or a candid observation. Skip throat-clearing such
as “In today's fast-paced world”.

### Benefits

Follow each feature through its consequences until it reaches something the
reader values:

```text
capability → immediate effect → practical outcome → emotional or financial value
```

Write from the lowest supported level. Do not claim an emotional or financial
result the evidence cannot support.

### Pain and cost

Quantify the status quo when reliable inputs exist. Show the assumptions behind
the calculation. Specific math is useful; invented precision is not.

### Proof

Use the strongest relevant evidence available: a demonstrated mechanism, real
example, measured result, customer account, or clear limitation. Place proof
near the claim it supports.

### Testimonials

Strong testimonials contain some combination of the before state, action,
specific outcome, timeframe, and reaction. Preserve the customer's actual
meaning and words. Do not ask the model to invent a quote-shaped placeholder;
label missing proof as missing proof.

### Calls to action

Name the action and make the next step predictable. Match commitment to reader
readiness. “Start free trial” is clearer than “Get started” when that is what the
button does.

### Momentum

Use unanswered questions or delayed details only when the eventual payoff is
real and nearby. Do not litter a page with vague teasers. Each section should
earn the next one through relevance, not tricks.

## Final landing-page check

- Does each major line come from a different insight rather than synonym cycling?
- Is the customer language recognizable and properly attributed?
- Are all claims and numbers supported?
- Does the page explain why this product, for this reader, now?
- Does each feature connect to a concrete benefit?
- Is the primary action obvious and accurately labeled?
- Could a real customer plausibly use this language?
- Does the hero put the outcome in a place and moment the reader recognizes?
- Do the example names and numbers stay consistent and add up across the page?
- Is everything not yet shipped labelled as such near the claim?
